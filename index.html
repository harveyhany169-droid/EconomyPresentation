PART 1

Macroeconomic Impact of Inflation

Lead Speaker: Sara Saber Samuel (2101138)

What is Inflation?

The broad upward movement of prices for goods and services in an economy.
Represents a decline in the purchasing power of money over time.
For engineers: Materials, labor, and machinery required for a project will inherently cost more in the future than today.

The Concept of Purchasing Power

Think of purchasing power as a 'basket of goods'.
>
If 100 units buys a certain amount of concrete today, that same 100 units will buy less concrete in five years due to inflation.
!
We cannot evaluate multi-year projects without accounting for this silent loss in value.

Understanding Interest Rates

Economists separate interest rates into two distinct types:

1. Real Interest Rate

The pure earning power of an investment. It measures actual growth in wealth, completely ignoring inflation.

2. Market (Nominal) Rate

The everyday rate seen at banks. It includes both the real earning power AND a buffer to cover expected inflation.

Types of Inflation in Engineering

General Inflation: The average price increase across the entire macroeconomy. (Our economic models primarily focus on adjusting for this).
Specific/Differential Inflation: Price changes for specific materials (like steel or copper) that may inflate faster or slower than the general economy.

Why Engineers Must Care

Underestimating Costs:
Ignoring inflation leads to massively underfunded projects.
Skewed Alternatives:
A project with high upfront costs might actually be cheaper long-term than a project with low upfront costs but highly inflating future maintenance costs.

PART 2

Present Worth Analysis Adjusted

Lead Speaker: Bishoy Tarek Soliman (2101067)

The Fundamental Interest Rate Equation

To calculate Present Worth (PW), we must link the Real Rate (i) and the Inflation Rate (f) to find the Market Rate (if).

if = i + f + (i * f)
Example: Real return (i) = 8%, Inflation (f) = 4% if = 12.32%

Categorizing Cash Flows

Before finding PW, we must classify our estimates:

Constant-Value (Real)

Estimates made using today's purchasing power (e.g., 'This machine costs 10k today').

Then-Current (Actual)

The actual inflated amount of cash/currency that will physically change hands in the future.

The Golden Rule of Inflation Analysis

RULE 1
If cash flows are Constant-Value, discount them to PW using the Real Interest Rate (i).
RULE 2
If cash flows are Then-Current (Actual), discount them to PW using the Market Interest Rate (if).
WARNING: Mixing these up is the most common error in engineering economy.

PW Method 1: Deflating Actual Values

If we know future actual costs, we use the market rate to find Present Worth directly.

PW =
Actual (1 + if)n

This single calculation simultaneously strips out the inflation effect and accounts for the time value of money.

PW Method 2: Two-Step Conversion

We can also find PW by breaking it into two distinct operations:

1. Remove Inflation

Find Constant Value:

CV =
Actual (1 + f)n

2. Discount to Present

Use Real Rate:

PW =
CV (1 + i)n

Both Method 1 and Method 2 yield the exact same Present Worth.

PART 3

Future Worth Projections & Inflation

Lead Speaker: Ziad Ahmed Anter (2101566)

Future Worth & Replacement Studies

Future Worth (FW) analysis is critical when planning for equipment replacement.
If an asset has a 10-year life cycle, we must know exactly how much capital we need in Year 10 to replace it.
Relying on today's prices guarantees a capital shortfall.
[ VISUAL: Glowing blueprint of a machine with a ghosted 'Future Replacement Model' overlaid ]

Projecting Then-Current Costs

To find the actual capital required in the future, we must artificially inflate today's cost estimate.

Future Actual Cost = Present Constant * (1 + f)^n
This calculation provides the strict target amount for establishing corporate Sinking Funds.

The Purchasing Power Formula

Mathematically proving how much value future money loses.

Power = 1 / (1 + f)^n
Example: At 5% inflation over 10 years, the formula yields ~0.61.

This means 1 unit in 10 years will only buy what 0.61 units buys today.
[ GRAPHIC: Digital fuel gauge dropping from 100% capacity down to 61% ]

Calculating Future Worth of Capital

When investing present capital to meet future inflated liabilities, we use the Market Rate (if).

FW = PW * (1 + if)^n

The market rate ensures our invested capital outpaces the inflation rate.

The Risk of Ignoring Inflation in FW

Scenario: A company saves 100k for a replacement based on today's prices.
Condition: Inflation averages 6% over 8 years.
Actual Cost: 100,000 * (1.06)^8 = 159,384.
Result: Firm is left with a massive ~60,000 unit deficit.

PART 4

Analytical Application: Hand-Calculated Case Study

Lead Speaker: Harvey Hany Makram (2100914)

Problem Statement (Equipment Lifecycle)

A manufacturing plant is purchasing a CNC machine for 50,000 units.
AOC
Annual Operating Cost estimated at 10,000 in today's constant units.
LIFE
Machine has a 4-year life.
RATES
Real MARR (i) is 8%, expected inflation (f) is 5%.
TASK: Calculate the total Present Worth of the machine's lifecycle.
[ HUD SCHEMATIC: 3D wireframe of a CNC milling machine with technical dimension lines ]

Step 1: Determine Market Rate (if)

Applying the linkage equation to find correct interest rate:

i = 0.08 | f = 0.05
if = i + f + (i * f)
if = 0.08 + 0.05 + (0.08 * 0.05) = 13.4%

Step 2: Projecting Actual AOC (Then-Current)

The 10,000 AOC must be inflated year by year using f = 5%:

YR 1
10,000 * (1.05)^1 = 10,500
YR 2
10,000 * (1.05)^2 = 11,025
YR 3
10,000 * (1.05)^3 = 11,576
YR 4
10,000 * (1.05)^4 = 12,155

Step 3: Discounting Actual to PW

Discount the Actual values using the Market Rate (if = 13.4%):

PW1 = 10,500 / (1.134)^1 = 9,259
PW2 = 11,025 / (1.134)^2 = 8,573
PW3 = 11,576 / (1.134)^3 = 7,938
PW4 = 12,155 / (1.134)^4 = 7,350
Total PW of AOC = 33,120

Step 4: The Constant-Dollar Shortcut

Verification using the Golden Rule: We can just use Constant Units (10k) and Real Rate (i = 8%).

PW = 10,000 * (P/A, 8%, 4)
PW = 10,000 * [(1.08^4 - 1) / (0.08 * 1.08^4)]
PW = 10,000 * 3.3121 = 33,121
Total Project PW = Initial Cost + PW(AOC)
50,000 + 33,121 = 83,121

PART 5

Computational Application: Spreadsheet Modeling

Lead Speaker: Mina Ezzat Soliman (2101363)

Translating the Math to a Spreadsheet

Spreadsheet modeling reduces human error in complex compounding calculations.
The model requires a dedicated 'Input Block' for variables: Real Rate (i), Inflation (f), and Life (N).
The Market Rate cell uses absolute references:

=$B$1+$B$2+($B$1*$B$2)
[ UI MOCKUP: Sleek dark-mode spreadsheet grid highlighting the Input Block ]

Building the Cash Flow Table

We set up columns for Year (0-4), Constant-Value Cash Flow, and Actual Cash Flow.

To generate Actual Cash Flow dynamically:
=Constant_Cell * (1 + $B$2)^Year_Cell
This automatically inflates the baseline costs down the column using the inflation parameter.

Applying Financial Functions

>
Excel's native =NPV() function handles the discounting.
SYN
Syntax: =NPV(rate, value1, [value2], ...)
CRITICAL
Point the rate argument to the calculated Market Rate (if) cell, and highlight the Actual Cash Flow column.
Total PW = Initial Cost + NPV(Market Rate, Actual Cash Flows).
[ UI MOCKUP: Spreadsheet showing the NPV formula selecting the Actual Cash Flow column ]

Validating the Model

To prove robustness, we run a secondary =NPV function using the Real Rate (i) against the Constant Cash Flow column.

NPV(Market Rate, Actual CF) ==== NPV(Real Rate, Constant CF)

Both outputs must match exactly. This acts as a built-in error check.

Conclusion & Summary

>
Inflation mathematically erodes purchasing power.
>
Market rates must be derived using: if = i + f + (i * f)
>
Constant strictly pairs with Real (i); Actual strictly pairs with Market (if).

FINAL DIRECTIVE

Ignoring inflation invalidates engineering economy studies and leads to catastrophic capital deficits.
